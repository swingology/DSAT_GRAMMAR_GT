# Verb Tense and Mood

> The SAT tests whether you can maintain **consistent tense** within a passage and whether you recognize the correct **mood** (indicative, subjunctive, imperative) for a given context. You don't need to name the moods — you need to hear when a sentence's timing or "vibe" shifts inconsistently.

---

## Verb Tenses: Quick Reference

| Tense | Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple present** | verb / verb+s | Habitual, general truth | *She **writes** every day.* |
| **Simple past** | verb+ed (or irregular) | Completed action | *She **wrote** the report.* |
| **Simple future** | will + verb | Future action | *She **will write** the report.* |
| **Present perfect** | has/have + past participle | Started in past, relevant now | *She **has written** three books.* |
| **Past perfect** | had + past participle | Completed before another past event | *She **had written** the draft before the meeting.* |
| **Future perfect** | will have + past participle | Completed before a future point | *She **will have written** it by Friday.* |
| **Present progressive** | is/are + -ing | Happening right now | *She **is writing** a novel.* |
| **Past progressive** | was/were + -ing | Ongoing in the past | *She **was writing** when I called.* |

See [[Participles]] for irregular past participle forms (gone, written, broken, etc.).

---

## Rule 1: Tense Consistency

If a paragraph or passage establishes a tense, **stay in that tense** unless there's a logical reason to shift (e.g., moving from a past narrative to a present-tense conclusion).

> ❌ The researcher **collected** data over three months and then **analyzes** the results.
> *(Shifts from past to present with no reason.)*
> ✅ The researcher **collected** data over three months and then **analyzed** the results.

> ❌ In 1969, Armstrong **walks** on the moon and **declared** it a giant leap for mankind.
> ✅ In 1969, Armstrong **walked** on the moon and **declared** it a giant leap for mankind.

### When Tense Shifts ARE Correct

Sometimes a shift is logical and necessary:

> ✅ The study, which **was conducted** in 2020, **suggests** that exercise improves cognition.
> *(The study was done in the past, but its findings are still relevant — present tense is correct for "suggests.")*

> ✅ Historians **have debated** this question for decades, and the disagreement **continues** today.
> *(Present perfect for the ongoing debate, present for the current state.)*

**💡 Learning Hint:** On the SAT, read the surrounding sentences to determine the established tense. If the underlined verb is in a different tense and there's no time signal (like "now," "yesterday," "in the future") justifying the shift, it's probably wrong.

---

## Rule 2: Past Perfect for the Earlier of Two Past Events

When two events both happened in the past, use **past perfect** (had + past participle) for the one that happened **first**.

> ❌ By the time we arrived, the concert **started**.
> ✅ By the time we arrived, the concert **had started**.
> *(The concert starting happened BEFORE the arrival.)*

> ❌ She realized she **left** her keys at home.
> ✅ She realized she **had left** her keys at home.
> *(The leaving happened BEFORE the realizing.)*

### Signal Words for Past Perfect

When you see these, consider whether past perfect is needed:

- **by the time**
- **before**
- **after** (sometimes)
- **already**
- **previously**
- **once**

## Rule 3: The Subjunctive Mood (Hypotheticals)

The subjunctive is used for situations that are **contrary to fact, hypothetical, or wished for**. The SAT tests one key pattern:

### "If I were" (not "If I was")

For hypothetical or contrary-to-fact "if" statements, use **were** for all subjects — including singular ones.

> ❌ If I **was** the captain, I would sail to the Grand Line.
> ✅ If I **were** the captain, I would sail to the Grand Line.

> ❌ If she **was** here, she would know what to do.
> ✅ If she **were** here, she would know what to do.

> ✅ If the policy **were** implemented, costs would decrease.

### How to Spot Subjunctive on the SAT

Look for the pattern: **"If [subject] were..., [subject] would/could/might..."**

The "would" in the second clause is your signal that this is hypothetical → use **were** in the "if" clause.

**Note:** If the "if" clause describes something that might actually be true, use normal tense:

> ✅ If she **was** at the meeting yesterday, she heard the announcement.
> *(This is possible — we're not sure if she was there. Not hypothetical.)*

### Subjunctive After "Wish"

> ❌ I wish I **was** taller.
> ✅ I wish I **were** taller. *(Contrary to fact — I'm not taller.)*

### Subjunctive in Demands / Recommendations

After verbs like *recommend, suggest, demand, insist, require*, use the **base form** of the verb (no -s, no -ed):

> ✅ The committee recommended that she **attend** the meeting.
> ❌ The committee recommended that she **attends** the meeting.

> ✅ The policy requires that every student **submit** an application.
> ❌ The policy requires that every student **submits** an application.

## Rule 4: The Imperative Mood (Commands)

Imperative sentences give commands or instructions. The subject ("you") is implied, not stated. The SAT tests this mainly in instructional contexts and [[Rhetorical Synthesis]] questions.

> ✅ **Preheat** the oven to 350°F.
> ✅ First, **crack** the eggs into a bowl.
> ✅ **Consider** the implications before deciding.

### Imperative Consistency

If a set of instructions begins in the imperative, all steps should remain in the imperative — don't switch to "you should" or a passive construction mid-list.

> ❌ First, **preheat** the oven. Then **you should mix** the ingredients. Finally, **pour** the batter.
> ✅ First, **preheat** the oven. Then **mix** the ingredients. Finally, **pour** the batter.

This is also a [[Parallelism]] issue — keeping verb forms consistent.

---

## Rule 5: Would / Will Consistency

Don't mix "would" (conditional/past) and "will" (future/certain) within the same context.

> ❌ She said she **will** attend the meeting, but she **would** need a ride.
> ✅ She said she **would** attend the meeting, but she **would** need a ride.

> ❌ If the weather improves, the team **would** play outside.
> ✅ If the weather improves, the team **will** play outside.
> *(Real possibility → "will," not "would.")*

---

## SAT Strategy: Verb Tense Questions

1. **Read the full paragraph** — identify the dominant tense
2. **Look for time signals** — dates, "now," "previously," "by the time"
3. **Check for sequence** — does one event happen before another? → consider past perfect
4. **Check for hypotheticals** — "if...would" → use subjunctive "were"
5. **Check for consistency** — does the underlined verb match the rest of the passage?

## Practice Questions

### Question 1

> By the time the rescue team arrived, the floodwaters already **rose** to dangerous levels.

A) NO CHANGE
B) have risen
C) had risen
D) are rising

**Correct Answer: C**
*Why?* The rising happened BEFORE the arrival (both past events). Past perfect ("had risen") marks the earlier event.

### Question 2

> If the city **was** to invest more in public transit, commute times would likely decrease.

A) NO CHANGE
B) were
C) is
D) will be

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* This is a hypothetical scenario (signaled by "would likely decrease" in the result clause). Use subjunctive "were."

### Question 3

> The professor insists that each student **submits** their work on time.

A) NO CHANGE
B) submit
C) will submit
D) submitted

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* After verbs of demand/insistence (insists, recommends, requires), use the base form "submit" — no -s ending.
