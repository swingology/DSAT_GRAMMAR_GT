# Sentence Boundaries and Clause Joining

> The most common errors on the SAT involve how **independent and dependent clauses** are joined. If you master these rules, you can answer a huge percentage of SAT grammar questions correctly.

See [[00_Essential Grammar Terms]] for definitions of independent and dependent clauses.

---

## Quick Review: Clause Types

| Type | Definition | Can Stand Alone? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Independent Clause (IC)** | Has a subject + verb and expresses a complete thought | ✅ Yes | *She studied all night.* |
| **Dependent Clause (DC)** | Has a subject + verb but starts with a subordinating word | ❌ No | *Because she studied all night...* |

**Common subordinating words** (these make a clause dependent): because, although, while, since, if, when, before, after, unless, until, whereas, even though, so that, as

---

## The 4 Legal Ways to Join Two Independent Clauses

This is one of the most heavily tested concepts on the SAT. Memorize all four:

### 1. Period

> ***IC.+ IC.***
> *She studied all night . She passed the exam.*

### 2. Semicolon

> ***IC;*** + ***IC.***
> *She studied all night**;** she passed the exam.*

The semicolon works exactly like a period but signals a closer relationship between the two ideas. No capital letter after the semicolon (unless it's a proper noun).

### 3. Comma + FANBOYS

> IC**,** FANBOYS IC.
> *She studied all night**, so** she passed the exam.*

**FANBOYS** = **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o

You need **both** the comma **and** the coordinating conjunction. A comma alone is not enough (that's a comma splice). A FANBOYS word alone without a comma before it is also technically wrong when joining two full ICs.

### 4. Colon

> IC**:** IC.
> *She had one goal**:** she wanted to pass the exam.*

Use the colon when the second clause **explains, defines, or summarizes** the first. See [[Colons Semicolons and Dashes]] for detailed colon rules.

---

## The Comma Splice: The #1 Error to Avoid

A **comma splice** is joining two independent clauses with only a comma. It is **always wrong** on the SAT.

> ❌ She studied all night**,** she passed the exam.
> *(Two ICs joined by only a comma = comma splice)*

### How to Fix a Comma Splice

| Fix | Result |
|---|---|
| Add a period | *She studied all night. She passed the exam.* |
| Add a semicolon | *She studied all night; she passed the exam.* |
| Add a FANBOYS after the comma | *She studied all night, **and** she passed the exam.* |
| Make one clause dependent | ***Because** she studied all night, she passed the exam.* |

**💡 Learning Hint:** On the SAT, if you see two complete thoughts separated by just a comma, it's wrong — no matter how natural it sounds. Look for an answer that adds a period, semicolon, or FANBOYS.

---

## Joining Independent + Dependent Clauses

When one clause is dependent, the rules are different:

**DC + comma + IC** (dependent clause first → comma required)
> **Because she studied all night,** she passed the exam.

**IC + DC** (independent clause first → usually no comma)
> She passed the exam **because she studied all night.**

**💡 Learning Hint:** If the sentence starts with a subordinating word (because, although, while, etc.), you almost always need a comma before the independent clause that follows.

---

## Run-On Sentences

A run-on is two independent clauses mashed together with **no punctuation at all**.

> ❌ She studied all night she passed the exam.

This is less common on the SAT than comma splices, but it does appear. The fix is the same: add proper punctuation or a conjunction.

---

## Fragments

A **fragment** is a group of words that looks like a sentence but is missing a subject, a main verb, or a complete thought.

> ❌ Because she studied all night. *(Dependent clause — no independent clause to complete it.)*
> ❌ The student studying for the SAT. *(No main verb — see [[Participles]] for the fragment trap.)*
> ❌ Running through the park on a sunny day. *(No subject and no main verb.)*

### Common Fragment Patterns on the SAT

| Pattern | Why It's a Fragment | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with a subordinating word | "Although the team practiced hard." | Add an IC: "Although the team practiced hard, **they lost**." |
| -ing word without a helper | "The committee reviewing the data." | Add a verb: "The committee **was** reviewing the data." |
| Appositive standing alone | "A talented chef with years of experience." | Attach to a sentence or add a verb |
| Relative clause alone | "Which was built in 1920." | Attach to preceding sentence |

## SAT Decision Flowchart: Sentence Boundaries

```
Look at what's on each side of the punctuation mark.

Is the left side a complete sentence (IC)?
       /            \
     YES             NO
      |               |
  Is the right       Is it a fragment
  side also an IC?   or dependent clause?
    /       \         → Different rules apply
  YES       NO        (see DC + IC patterns)
   |         |
  How are    A comma alone
  they       is fine here
  joined?    (IC, DC is legal)
   |
  Period ✅
  Semicolon ✅
  Comma + FANBOYS ✅
  Colon (if explains) ✅
  Comma alone ❌ (SPLICE!)
  Nothing ❌ (RUN-ON!)
```

## Practice Questions

### Question 1

> The museum's new exhibit features artifacts from ancient Rome, it has attracted visitors from around the world.

A) NO CHANGE
B) Rome; it has attracted
C) Rome it has attracted
D) Rome, or it has attracted

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* The original is a comma splice (two ICs joined by only a comma). B correctly uses a semicolon. C is a run-on. D changes the meaning with "or."

### Question 2

> Although the experiment yielded promising results. The researchers decided to conduct additional trials before publishing.

A) NO CHANGE
B) results, the researchers
C) results; the researchers
D) results: the researchers

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* "Although the experiment yielded promising results" is a dependent clause — it can't stand alone with a period. B correctly joins DC + comma + IC. C and D are wrong because a semicolon and colon both require an IC on the left side.
