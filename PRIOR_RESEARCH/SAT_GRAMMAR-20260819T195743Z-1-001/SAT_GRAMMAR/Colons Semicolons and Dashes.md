# Colons, Semicolons, and Dashes

> The SAT tests whether you know the specific rules for each punctuation mark. They are **not interchangeable** — each has precise conditions for correct use.

---

## The Colon ( : )

### The Rule

The part **before** the colon **must be a complete independent clause**. What comes after can be a list, a quote, an explanation, or even another independent clause.

### Correct Uses

**Introducing a list:**
> ✅ She packed three things**:** a tent, a sleeping bag, and a flashlight.
> ❌ She packed**:** a tent, a sleeping bag, and a flashlight.
> *(The part before the colon — "She packed" — isn't a complete thought when followed by a colon introducing what she packed. Needs "three things" or similar to complete it.)*

**Introducing an explanation:**
> ✅ He had one goal**:** to win the championship.
> ✅ The results were clear**:** the new method was more effective.

**Introducing a quote:**
> ✅ The coach gave simple advice**:** "Play your best."

### The SAT Trap

> ❌ His goals were**:** to win and to play hard.
> *(The part before the colon — "His goals were" — is incomplete when the colon interrupts before the predicate nominative. Fix: "He had two goals: to win and to play hard.")*

> ❌ The ingredients include**:** flour, sugar, and eggs.
> ✅ The recipe requires several ingredients**:** flour, sugar, and eggs.

**💡 Learning Hint:** Cover everything after the colon. Can what remains stand alone as a sentence? If yes → colon is legal. If no → colon is wrong.

## The Semicolon ( ; )

### The Rules

**Rule 1: Joins two independent clauses** (same as [[Sentence Boundaries and Clause Joining]])
> ✅ The experiment failed**;** the team decided to try again.
> *(Both sides must be complete sentences.)*

**Rule 2: Separates items in a list that already contains commas**
> ✅ The team visited Paris, France**;** London, England**;** and Tokyo, Japan.
> *(Without semicolons, the commas would be confusing.)*

### What a Semicolon Is NOT

- ❌ It is NOT a substitute for a colon before a list
- ❌ It is NOT used between an IC and a DC
- ❌ It is NOT used after a greeting (that's a colon)

> ❌ She needed several items**;** a pen, paper, and tape.
> ✅ She needed several items**:** a pen, paper, and tape.

> ❌ Although she tried hard**;** she couldn't finish in time.
> ✅ Although she tried hard**,** she couldn't finish in time.
> *("Although she tried hard" is a DC, not an IC.)*

**💡 Learning Hint:** A semicolon works exactly like a period — it needs a complete sentence on **both** sides. If you wouldn't put a period there, don't put a semicolon there.

## The Dash ( — )

Dashes are more flexible than colons and semicolons, but the SAT tests two specific patterns:

### Single Dash

Functions like a colon — it adds emphasis, an explanation, or a dramatic afterthought at the end of a sentence.

> ✅ She had only one fear**—**failure.
> ✅ The answer was obvious**—**no one had checked the data.

The part before the dash must be an independent clause (same rule as colons).

### Double Dashes (Paired)

Function like parentheses — they interrupt a sentence with **non-essential information**. The sentence must be grammatically complete if you remove everything between the dashes.

> ✅ The scientist**—**who had won a Nobel Prize**—**lectured today.
> *(Remove the dashed section: "The scientist lectured today." ✅ Complete.)*

> ✅ My grandmother's recipe**—**a closely guarded family secret**—**won first place.
> *(Remove: "My grandmother's recipe won first place." ✅ Complete.)*

### The SAT Trap: Matching Punctuation

If a non-essential phrase starts with a dash, it **must** end with a dash (not a comma or parenthesis). The SAT tests whether the punctuation marks match.

> ❌ The scientist**—**who had won a Nobel Prize**,** lectured today.
> *(Mismatched: dash opens, comma closes.)*
> ✅ The scientist**—**who had won a Nobel Prize**—**lectured today.

The same rule applies to commas and parentheses: they must match in pairs.

> ❌ The scientist (who had won a Nobel Prize**,** lectured today.
> ✅ The scientist (who had won a Nobel Prize) lectured today.
> ✅ The scientist, who had won a Nobel Prize, lectured today.

---

## Commas with Non-Essential Phrases

If you can **remove a phrase** from a sentence and it still makes grammatical sense, that phrase is **non-essential** and must be "hugged" by commas (or dashes, or parentheses).

> ✅ Luffy**,** the captain of the crew**,** loves meat.
> *(Remove "the captain of the crew" → "Luffy loves meat." Still works.)*

> ✅ The Eiffel Tower**,** which was built in 1889**,** attracts millions of visitors.
> *(Remove "which was built in 1889" → sentence still works.)*

### Essential vs. Non-Essential (Restrictive vs. Non-Restrictive)

| Type | Commas? | Test | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Non-essential** (extra info) | ✅ Yes, commas | Remove it — sentence still complete and meaning unchanged | *My brother, **who lives in Boston**, is visiting.* (I only have one brother.) |
| **Essential** (identifies which one) | ❌ No commas | Remove it — meaning changes or becomes unclear | *The student **who scored highest** won the award.* (Which student? This one.) |

### "That" vs. "Which"

- **That** → introduces essential (restrictive) clauses → NO commas
- **Which** → introduces non-essential (non-restrictive) clauses → YES commas

> ✅ The book **that** I borrowed is overdue. *(essential — which book?)*
> ✅ The book**,** **which** I borrowed last week**,** is overdue. *(non-essential — extra info)*

**💡 Learning Hint:** On the SAT, if a phrase is surrounded by commas (or dashes), mentally delete it. If the remaining sentence is complete and the meaning hasn't fundamentally changed, the commas are correct. If it breaks the sentence, the commas shouldn't be there.

## Quick Comparison Table

| Mark | Requires IC before it? | What can follow? | Primary use |
|---|---|---|---|
| **.** Period | ✅ | IC | End a sentence |
| **;** Semicolon | ✅ (both sides) | IC only | Join related ICs |
| **:** Colon | ✅ | IC, list, quote, explanation | Introduce/explain |
| **—** Single dash | ✅ | Anything | Emphasis, explanation |
| **— —** Paired dashes | N/A | Non-essential info inside | Interrupt with extra info |
| **,** Comma | Varies | Varies | Many uses (see [[Comma Rules]]) |

## Practice Questions

### Question 1

> The chef prepared three courses for the dinner party, a salad, grilled salmon, and chocolate mousse.

A) NO CHANGE
B) party: a salad,
C) party; a salad,
D) party—a salad,

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* A list is being introduced. The part before the punctuation ("The chef prepared three courses for the dinner party") is a complete IC. A colon correctly introduces the list. A semicolon (C) can't introduce a list. The original (A) creates a comma splice/run-on.

### Question 2

> The mountain range—which spans over 4,000 miles, is home to diverse ecosystems.

A) NO CHANGE
B) miles—is home
C) miles; is home
D) miles, is home

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* The non-essential phrase starts with a dash, so it must end with a dash. A creates mismatched punctuation (dash opens, comma closes).

### Question 3 (High Difficulty — Colon vs. Semicolon vs. Comma)

> The research team identified the primary catalyst for the chemical reaction _________ a rare enzyme discovered in the depths of the Amazon rainforest.

A) catalyst;
B) catalyst:
C) catalyst,
D) catalyst

**Correct Answer: B**
*Why?* The phrase after the blank explains exactly what the catalyst is — "a rare enzyme..." is a definition/explanation. A colon correctly introduces this. A semicolon (A) is wrong because the second part ("a rare enzyme...") is **not** a complete sentence — it's a noun phrase. A comma (C) creates an ambiguous appositive without proper setup. D (no punctuation) is a run-on. This question tests whether you can distinguish the three marks when all seem plausible.
