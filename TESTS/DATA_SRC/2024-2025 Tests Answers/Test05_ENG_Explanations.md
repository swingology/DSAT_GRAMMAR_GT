## Module 1, Q18 — Ever Given / Suez Canal
**Type: Standard English Conventions — Punctuation (Colon vs Comma)**
**Answer: B** — *dislodge: in part due to its sheer size,*

**How it works:**
The blank follows "dislodge" and introduces an explanatory clause about *why* the ship was so hard to free. A colon is the correct punctuation to introduce an explanation or elaboration of what precedes it. The second half of the sentence ("in part due to its sheer size, it had become lodged in the mud") directly explains the difficulty of dislodging the vessel.

**Distractor breakdown:**

| Option | Text | Why it fails |
|--------|------|-------------|
| A | dislodge; in part due to its sheer size, | A semicolon connects two independent clauses — "in part due to its sheer size, it had become lodged in the mud" is not independent without restructuring |
| **B** ✅ | dislodge: in part due to its sheer size, | Colon correctly introduces the explanatory phrase |
| C | dislodge, in part due to its sheer size, | **TRAP** — see below |
| D | dislodge in part due to its sheer size, | No punctuation — runs the explanation into the verb with no signal |

**Trap: C** — Students who correctly identify that a comma belongs after "dislodge" select this. It looks right because it creates a natural pause. The error: the second half of the sentence is an explanatory clause, not a continuation, so a colon is required to signal that relationship. Students who hear the comma as a "pause" but don't analyze the logical relationship between clauses will pick C.

## Module 1, Q20 — Willard Wigan's Microsculptures
**Type: Standard English Conventions — Sentence Boundaries**
**Answer: C** — *microsculptures. Creations*

**How it works:**
The period after "microsculptures" ends the first sentence cleanly. The second sentence opens with an introductory absolute phrase — *"Creations so small that they are best viewed through a microscope,"* — followed by a comma, then the main clause: *"Wigan's sculptures are made from tiny natural materials, such as spiderweb strands."* Structure: introductory phrase + comma + main clause.

**Distractor breakdown:**

| Option | Text | Why it fails |
|--------|------|-------------|
| A | microsculptures creations | Two nouns with no punctuation — immediately ungrammatical |
| **B** | microsculptures, creations | **TRAP** — see below |
| **C** ✅ | microsculptures. Creations | Correct sentence boundary; absolute phrase correctly introduces next sentence |
| D | microsculptures and creations | Compound noun is plausible, but "Wigan's sculptures are made from..." becomes a comma splice |

**Trap: B** — Students correctly recognize that "creations" renames "microsculptures" (appositive logic) and add a comma. It sounds right. The problem: once the appositive closes ("...through a microscope,"), the next clause "Wigan's sculptures are made from tiny natural materials" is a new independent clause — creating a comma splice. The appositive comma does not fix the sentence boundary; only a period does.

## Module 1, Q23 — Lewis Latimer / Electric Light Bulb
**Type: Transitions — Elaboration / Specification**
**Answer: D** — *Specifically,*

**How it works:**
Sentence 1 makes a broad claim: Latimer *improved upon* Edison's design. Sentence 2 gives the exact mechanism: placing cardboard around the carbon filament. The relationship is **general → specific**. "Specifically" signals "here is the precise detail of what I just said."

**Distractor breakdown:**

| Option | Text | Why it fails |
|--------|------|-------------|
| A | Soon, | Temporal — implies a time gap; the second sentence IS the improvement, not something that happened after it |
| B | Regardless, | Concessive — implies overcoming an obstacle; there is no obstacle or opposing idea in context |
| **C** | However, | **TRAP** — see below |
| **D** ✅ | Specifically, | Correct — introduces the specific mechanism behind the general improvement |

**Trap: C** — Students read a slight tonal shift between "improved upon Edison's design" and "made the bulb more durable" and interpret it as contrast — as if durability is somehow *different from* or *in spite of* the improvement. But both sentences describe the same action. There is no contrast; "however" requires two opposing ideas. Students who mistake elaboration for contrast will pick C.

## Module 2, Q18 — Louise Bennett
**Type: Standard English Conventions — Punctuation (Comma After Verb)**
**Answer: C** — *popularized*

**How it works:**
The blank fills the verb position in the sentence. The verb "popularized" takes a direct object ("Jamaican Creole as a literary language") — no punctuation should separate a verb from its direct object. The sentence reads cleanly: "Bennett popularized Jamaican Creole as a literary language."

**Distractor breakdown:**

| Option | Text | Why it fails |
|--------|------|-------------|
| A | popularized; | Semicolon separates two independent clauses — the direct object that follows is not an independent clause |
| **B** | popularized, | **TRAP** — see below |
| **C** ✅ | popularized | Correct — no punctuation between verb and direct object |
| D | popularized: | A colon introduces an explanation or list — the direct object that follows is neither |

**Trap: B** — The comma after "popularized" looks plausible to students who over-apply comma rules or mistake "Jamaican Creole as a literary language" for an appositive or introductory phrase. It creates a natural-sounding pause. The error: commas never separate a verb from its direct object. Students who apply comma rules by "feel" rather than function will pick B.
