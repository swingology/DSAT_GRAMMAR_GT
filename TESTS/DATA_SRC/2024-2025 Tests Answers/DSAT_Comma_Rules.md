# DSAT — Complete Comma Grammar Rules, Distractors & Traps

All rules the Digital SAT tests under Standard English Conventions (SEC).
Each section includes: the rule, correct structure, distractor types, and the trap.

---

## 1. COMMA SPLICE
**Rule:** Two independent clauses cannot be joined by a comma alone.

**Correct structures:**
- Period: `IC. IC.`
  - *Easy:* The dog barked loudly. The mail carrier dropped the package.
  - *Hard:* The initial phase of the project required an unprecedented level of interdepartmental coordination. By the third quarter, however, the logistical challenges had been completely resolved.
- Semicolon: `IC; IC.`
  - *Easy:* The storm passed quickly; the sun came out.
  - *Hard:* The researchers hypothesized that the new compound would accelerate the reaction; subsequent trials revealed it actually functioned as an inhibitor.
- Comma + coordinating conjunction: `IC, [for/and/nor/but/or/yet/so] IC.`
  - *Easy:* The team collected the data, and they published their findings.
  - *Hard:* The committee spent several months reviewing the proposed changes to the zoning laws, but they ultimately decided to reject the measure. *(Long clauses can disguise the IC structure)*
- Subordinating conjunction (no comma needed): `because/although/since IC, IC.`
  - *Easy:* Because the experiment failed, the team started over.
  - *Hard:* Although the initial spectroscopic analysis suggested the presence of the elusive isotope, subsequent testing with a more sensitive calibration revealed it to be a common contaminant.

**Wrong:**
> The experiment failed, the team started over.

**Right:**
> The experiment failed. The team started over.
> The experiment failed, so the team started over.

**Distractors:**
- Adding a semicolon but keeping a coordinating conjunction: `IC; and IC` — wrong (semicolon replaces the conjunction, not joins with it)
- Using a comma + transitional adverb: `IC, however, IC` — still a splice; "however" is not a coordinating conjunction

**Trap:** The comma *feels* natural as a spoken pause between related ideas. Students who read for rhythm rather than structure will miss the splice every time.

---

## 2. COMMAS WITH COORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS (FANBOYS)
**Rule:** Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) **only when both sides are independent clauses**.

**Correct:**
> She studied all night, and she passed the exam. *(both sides = IC)*

**Wrong:**
> She studied all night, and passed the exam. *(second side = just a verb phrase, not IC)*

**Distractors:**
- Adding a comma before "and/but" when the second element is just a predicate or verb phrase (over-punctuation)
- Omitting the comma when both sides genuinely are independent clauses

**Trap:** Students add commas before "and" reflexively. The test frequently pairs a long first clause with a short second predicate — the length difference makes it *look* like two independent clauses, but the second has no subject.

---

## 3. COMMAS WITH INTRODUCTORY ELEMENTS
**Rule:** An introductory word, phrase, or clause at the start of a sentence is followed by a comma.

**Types of introductory elements:**
- Adverbial clause: `Although she was tired, she continued.`
- Participial phrase: `Running down the hall, she dropped her keys.`
- Prepositional phrase: `In the early 1900s, jazz emerged in New Orleans.`
- Absolute phrase: `Her work complete, she left the office.`
- Transitional word: `However, the results were inconclusive.`

**Wrong:**
> Running down the hall she dropped her keys.

**Distractors:**
- Omitting the comma after the introductory element entirely
- Adding a comma *inside* the introductory phrase instead of after it
- `Running, down the hall she dropped her keys.` — comma in wrong position

**Trap:** When the introductory phrase is long, students sometimes place the comma in the middle of the phrase (at the first natural pause) rather than at the end of the full phrase.

---

## 4. COMMAS WITH APPOSITIVES
**Rule:** A non-restrictive appositive (a noun phrase that renames the noun before it) is set off by commas. A restrictive appositive (essential to identify which one) takes no commas.

**Non-restrictive (extra info, removable):**
> My sister, a marine biologist, studies coral reefs. *(commas on both sides)*

**Restrictive (essential, identifies which one):**
> My sister Claire studies coral reefs. *(no commas — "Claire" specifies which sister)*

**Common DSAT structure:** `[Subject], [appositive phrase], [verb]...`

**Distractors:**
- Only one comma instead of two when the appositive is mid-sentence: `My sister, a marine biologist studies coral reefs.`
- Using commas for a restrictive appositive (changes meaning by implying there's only one)
- No commas when the appositive is clearly non-restrictive

**Trap:** In long sentences the second comma (closing the appositive) is frequently the one that gets dropped. The opening comma looks correct, so students stop checking.

---

## 5. COMMA SEPARATING SUBJECT FROM VERB
**Rule:** Never put a comma between a subject and its verb, regardless of how long the subject is.

**Wrong:**
> The tall woman in the red coat, waved to us.
> Researchers who study migratory birds, have found new patterns.

**Right:**
> The tall woman in the red coat waved to us.

**Distractors:**
- Adding a comma after a long or complex subject to signal a "pause before the action"
- Adding a comma after a subject that ends with a prepositional phrase or relative clause

**Trap:** The longer the subject, the more a student *feels* a comma is needed before the verb. The test deliberately uses multi-line subjects to exploit this instinct.

---

## 6. COMMA SEPARATING VERB FROM DIRECT OBJECT
**Rule:** Never put a comma between a verb and its direct object or complement.

**Wrong:**
> She popularized, Jamaican Creole as a literary language.
> The study revealed, that temperatures had risen.

**Right:**
> She popularized Jamaican Creole as a literary language.

**Distractors:**
- Comma after a verb when the object is a long noun phrase
- Comma after a verb when the object is a "that" clause

**Trap:** When the direct object is a long phrase or clause, students insert a comma to mark the boundary — treating it like an introductory element. This is the "feels like a pause" error.

---

## 7. COMMAS WITH NON-RESTRICTIVE (WHICH) CLAUSES
**Rule:** Non-restrictive relative clauses — those beginning with "which" — are set off by commas. Restrictive clauses — those beginning with "that" — take no commas.

**Non-restrictive (extra info):**
> The report, which was released last month, contradicts earlier findings.

**Restrictive (essential):**
> The report that was released last month contradicts earlier findings. *(specifies which report)*

**Distractors:**
- Using "which" without commas (treats non-restrictive clause as restrictive)
- Using "that" with commas (adds unnecessary commas around essential clause)
- Only one comma when the clause is mid-sentence

**Trap:** Students sometimes use "which" and "that" interchangeably, then apply commas inconsistently. The test exploits this by using contexts where the distinction changes the meaning significantly.

---

## 8. COMMAS WITH PARTICIPIAL PHRASES (MID-SENTENCE)
**Rule:** A participial phrase that modifies the subject and is inserted mid-sentence is set off by commas on both sides.

**Correct:**
> The scientist, having reviewed all the data, submitted her findings.
> The bridge, built in 1887, still carries daily traffic.

**Wrong:**
> The scientist, having reviewed all the data submitted her findings. *(missing closing comma)*

**Distractors:**
- Missing the second comma (closing comma after the phrase)
- Placing commas around a restrictive participial phrase that is essential to meaning

**Trap:** Same as appositives — the closing comma is the one that gets dropped. The test often places the participial phrase near the end of a long line so the second comma is visually far from the first.

---

## 9. COMMAS WITH ABSOLUTE PHRASES
**Rule:** An absolute phrase (noun + participle, no helping verb) is set off by a comma from the main clause.

**Introductory:**
> Her research complete, she submitted the paper.
> The storm having passed, the crew resumed work.

**Closing:**
> She submitted the paper, her research finally complete.

**Distractors:**
- No comma between absolute phrase and main clause
- Confusing absolute phrase with participial phrase (absolute phrase has its own noun subject; participial phrase modifies the sentence's subject)

**Trap:** Absolute phrases are rarer and less taught, so students don't recognize the structure and either drop the comma entirely or treat it as a sentence fragment.

---

## 10. COMMAS IN LISTS / SERIES
**Rule:** Items in a series of three or more are separated by commas. The SAT uses the Oxford comma (comma before the final "and").

**Correct:**
> She studied history, biology, and chemistry.

**Wrong (missing Oxford comma):**
> She studied history, biology and chemistry.

**Semicolons in complex lists:** When list items themselves contain commas, use semicolons to separate items.
> The team included Dr. Lee, a biologist; Dr. Park, a chemist; and Dr. Kim, a physicist.

**Distractors:**
- Using semicolons in a simple list where no item contains a comma (over-punctuation)
- Using commas in a complex list where items already contain commas (under-punctuation/ambiguity)
- Missing the Oxford comma

**Trap:** The test presents a list with two items plus a compound last item ("A, B and C and D") — students must recognize whether the "and" is joining list items or joining two parts of the final item.

---

## 11. COMMA vs COLON
**Rule:** A colon introduces an explanation, elaboration, or list. A comma cannot do this work alone.

**Use a colon when:**
- The second part explains or elaborates on the first: `He had one goal: to win.`
- The second part is a list: `She packed three things: a map, a compass, and water.`
- The first clause could end with "namely" or "that is"

**Wrong (comma instead of colon):**
> The vessel was difficult to dislodge, in part due to its sheer size, it had become stuck in the mud.

**Right:**
> The vessel was difficult to dislodge: in part due to its sheer size, it had become stuck in the mud.

**Distractors:**
- Comma before an explanatory phrase (sounds like a natural pause, feels correct)
- Semicolon before an explanatory phrase (semicolons join clauses, don't introduce explanations)

**Trap:** The comma before an explanatory clause is the most common error on colon questions. It feels natural to pause before "in part because..." or "specifically because..." — but the logical relationship requires a colon, not a comma.

---

## 12. COMMA vs SEMICOLON
**Rule:** A semicolon joins two independent clauses. It signals a close logical relationship but does not specify what that relationship is (contrast, sequence, elaboration). A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses.

**Correct:**
> The results were unexpected; the team revised their hypothesis.

**Wrong:**
> The results were unexpected, the team revised their hypothesis. *(comma splice)*

**Distractors:**
- Semicolon + coordinating conjunction: `IC; and IC` — wrong; the semicolon replaces the conjunction
- Comma + transitional adverb without a conjunction: `IC, however, IC` — still a comma splice

**Trap:** Students learn "semicolons join clauses" and then add one wherever they see two clauses — including after transitional phrases that already use a comma, resulting in `IC; however, IC` (correct) vs `IC, however, IC` (splice).

---

## 13. COMMAS WITH TRANSITIONAL ADVERBS
**Rule:** When a transitional adverb (however, therefore, moreover, consequently, furthermore, nevertheless) begins a new clause after a period or semicolon, it is followed by a comma. When inserted mid-sentence, it is set off by commas on both sides.

**Beginning of new sentence:**
> The data was incomplete. However, the team drew preliminary conclusions.

**Mid-sentence:**
> The team, however, drew preliminary conclusions.

**Wrong:**
> The data was incomplete, however the team drew preliminary conclusions. *(comma splice — "however" is not a coordinating conjunction)*

**Distractors:**
- Using a comma before "however" when it begins the second independent clause (creates splice)
- Omitting the comma after "however" at the start of a clause
- Using "however" mid-sentence without commas on both sides

**Trap:** Students treat "however" like "but" — a coordinating conjunction — and write `IC, however IC`. But "however" is a conjunctive adverb; it cannot join clauses with just a comma.

---

## 14. EXTRA / UNNECESSARY COMMAS (Over-Punctuation)
**Rule:** Do not add commas between elements that function together as a unit.

**Never add commas:**
- Between a verb and its object: `She ~~,~~ studied biology.`
- Between a subject and its verb: `The tall student ~~,~~ won.`
- Between an adjective and the noun it modifies: `a beautiful ~~,~~ painting`
- Between two adjectives where the second is a closer modifier: `a small ~~,~~ red car` (no comma — "red car" is a unit)
- Inside a prepositional phrase: `in the ~~,~~ morning`
- Between a conjunction and the clause it introduces: `because ~~,~~ she was tired`

**Distractors:**
- Comma after the subject when it ends with a modifying clause
- Comma between a coordinating adjective and the noun: `It was a long, difficult, ~~,~~ journey.`

**Trap:** Two coordinating adjectives (both independently modify the noun) do take a comma: `a long, difficult journey.` But "small red car" takes no comma because "red" modifies "car" and "small" modifies "red car" as a unit. Students apply the comma-between-adjectives rule without checking whether it applies.

---

## 15. COORDINATE vs. CUMULATIVE ADJECTIVES
**Rule:** Use a comma between two **coordinate** adjectives (each independently modifies the noun; order can be reversed). Do NOT use a comma between **cumulative** adjectives (they build on each other; reversing them sounds wrong).

**Test:** Can you reverse the adjectives and still have them sound natural? Can you insert "and" between them naturally?

**Coordinate (comma required):**
> a warm, active animal *(→ "an active, warm animal" also works; "warm and active" works)*

**Cumulative (no comma):**
> a large gray whale *(→ "a gray large whale" sounds wrong)*
> a small red car *(→ "a red small car" sounds wrong)*

**Distractors:**
- Inserting a comma between cumulative adjectives: `a large, gray whale` — wrong
- Omitting the comma between genuine coordinate adjectives: `a warm active animal` — wrong

**Trap:** Students apply the adjective comma rule by feel rather than by the reversibility test. The test pairs two descriptive adjectives where one is a type/color/material (cumulative) and the other is a quality (coordinate) — they look identical but only one pattern takes a comma.

---

## 16. PAIRED PUNCTUATION SYMMETRY (THE "0 OR 2" RULE)
**Rule:** When a non-essential element (appositive, parenthetical, interrupter) is inserted mid-sentence, it must be enclosed by a **matching pair** of the same punctuation type — either **two commas**, **two dashes**, or **two parentheses**. You cannot mix types. You cannot use only one.

**Correct:**
> The scientist, a renowned chemist, published her findings.
> The scientist — a renowned chemist — published her findings.

**Wrong (mismatched):**
> The scientist, a renowned chemist — published her findings.

**Wrong (only one):**
> The scientist, a renowned chemist published her findings.

**Distractors:**
- One comma opening, one dash closing (or vice versa)
- One comma with no closing mark at all
- Correct pair type but wrong number of marks

**Trap:** Students verify that punctuation is *present* around the non-essential element but don't check that the marks are *symmetrical and matching*. The distractor usually gets the opening mark right and then uses the wrong type or omits the closing mark entirely.

---

## 17. FORBIDDEN COMMA POSITIONS (Specific Words and Constructions)
**Rule:** Certain words and constructions never take a comma before or after them, regardless of sentence length or complexity.

### a. Never before "that"
A comma before "that" introducing a noun clause or relative clause is always wrong.

> Wrong: She knew, that the results were correct.
> Right: She knew that the results were correct.

### b. Never before or after a preposition (mid-clause)
A comma before a prepositional phrase that directly follows the main clause is wrong.

> Wrong: The committee met, in the morning.
> Right: The committee met in the morning.
*(Exception: an introductory prepositional phrase at the start of a sentence DOES take a comma after it — see Rule 3.)*

### c. Never after "such as," "like," or "including"
These words introduce examples directly and must not be followed by a comma.

> Wrong: Animals such as, dolphins and whales are mammals.
> Right: Animals such as dolphins and whales are mammals.

### d. Never in a two-item list
A two-item list joined by "and" or "or" requires no comma. The Oxford comma rule applies only to three or more items.

> Wrong: She studied biology, and chemistry.
> Right: She studied biology and chemistry.

**Trap:** Students apply the Oxford-comma rule to two-item lists after seeing it successfully applied to three-item lists nearby. The test frequently places a two-item list in a sentence that also contains other commas, priming students to add one more.

---

## 18. COMMA INSIDE QUOTATION MARKS
**Rule:** In American English convention, commas and periods always go **inside** the closing quotation mark — no exceptions.

**Correct:**
> She called it "a masterpiece," and then left the room.
> He said the answer was "unclear," though he kept looking.

**Wrong:**
> She called it "a masterpiece", and then left the room.

**Note:** Question marks and exclamation marks go inside only if they are part of the quoted material; otherwise they go outside. This rule applies only to commas and periods — they always go inside.

**Distractors:**
- Comma placed outside the closing quotation mark
- No comma at all after the quoted phrase when one is needed

**Trap:** Students who learned British punctuation conventions (where placement depends on whether the mark is part of the quote) will place the comma outside. Also, it looks visually cleaner to place the comma outside, which is why the distractor is seductive.

---

## 19. COMMA WITH SUBORDINATING CONJUNCTIONS (POSITION MATTERS)
**Rule:** When an adverbial clause comes *before* the main clause (introductory), use a comma after it. When it comes *after* the main clause, the comma depends on the type of conjunction.

**Before main clause → comma after the dependent clause:**
> Although she was tired, she continued working.
> Because the results were unclear, the team ran more tests.

**After main clause — causal conjunctions → NO comma:**
> She continued working because she was tired.
> The team ran more tests since the results were unclear.
*(because, since, so that, unless, until, when, after, before)*

**After main clause — concessive/contrast conjunctions → comma:**
> She continued working, although she was exhausted.
> The findings were significant, even though the sample was small.
*(although, even though, whereas, while used contrastively, though)*

**Distractors:**
- Inserting a comma before "because" when it follows the main clause: `She left, because she was tired.` — wrong
- Omitting the comma before "although/even though" when it follows the main clause
- Confusing "while" (concessive = needs comma) with "while" (temporal = no comma)

**Trap:** Students learn the introductory-clause rule (Rule 3) and apply it symmetrically — they think if a clause takes a comma when it comes first, it always takes a comma. The reversal only applies to concessive conjunctions, not causal ones. "Because" after a main clause never takes a comma; "although" after a main clause always does.

## Quick Reference: Comma Decision Tree

```
Two full sentences (each has subject + verb)?
  YES → Use period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS (not comma alone)
  NO  → Continue below

Is there an introductory element before the main clause?
  YES → Comma after the introductory element
  NO  → Continue below

Does a subordinate clause come AFTER the main clause?
  Causal (because, since, so that, unless) → NO comma
  Concessive (although, even though, whereas) → comma before it

Is there a non-essential element inserted mid-sentence?
  YES → Commas (or dashes, or parentheses) on BOTH sides — must match
  NO  → Continue below

Is there a noun phrase that renames/describes another noun?
  Removable (non-restrictive)? → Commas on both sides
  Essential (restrictive)?     → No commas

Is there a "which" clause? → Commas (non-restrictive)
Is there a "that" clause?  → No commas (restrictive), and no comma BEFORE "that"

Are there two adjectives before a noun?
  Reversible / "and" fits naturally? → Comma between them (coordinate)
  Order fixed / "and" sounds wrong?  → No comma (cumulative)

Does the sentence introduce an explanation or list?
  YES → Colon (not comma)

Is the word "however/therefore/moreover" joining two clauses?
  YES → Semicolon before it, comma after it (NOT comma before it)

Is there a list?
  Two items only → No comma before "and/or"
  Three or more  → Oxford comma (comma before final "and")
  Items contain internal commas → Use semicolons between items

Is there a quoted word or phrase followed by a comma?
  YES → Comma goes INSIDE the closing quotation mark

Does the sentence use "such as," "like," or "including"?
  YES → No comma after these words
```

## Most Common DSAT Traps Summary

| Trap | What Students Do | Why It's Wrong |
|------|-----------------|----------------|
| Comma splice | Join two ICs with just a comma | Comma alone cannot join ICs |
| Colon replaced by comma | Use comma before explanation | Comma doesn't signal elaboration; colon does |
| Appositive with one comma | Open appositive but don't close it | Appositive needs commas on both sides |
| "However" after comma | `IC, however IC` | "However" is not a coordinating conjunction |
| Comma before verb object | Add comma after long verb | Verb and object are never separated by comma |
| Comma after long subject | Add comma before verb | Subject and verb are never separated |
| "And" after comma (no IC) | Add comma before every "and" | Comma only needed before "and" if both sides are ICs |
| "Which" without commas | Use "which" like "that" | "Which" introduces non-restrictive clause → needs commas |
| Cumulative adjective comma | `large, gray whale` | Only coordinate (reversible) adjectives take a comma |
| Semicolon + conjunction | `IC; and IC` | Semicolon replaces conjunction, not pairs with it |
| Mismatched paired punctuation | Comma open, dash close | Non-essential elements need matching punctuation on both sides |
| Comma before "that" | `knew, that it was true` | "That" clauses never take a preceding comma |
| Comma after "such as" | `animals such as, dolphins` | "Such as/like/including" are never followed by a comma |
| Comma in two-item list | `biology, and chemistry` | Oxford comma only applies to three or more items |
| Comma outside quotation marks | `"a masterpiece", and` | Commas always go inside closing quotation marks |
| Comma before "because" | `she left, because` | Causal conjunctions after main clause take no comma |
| No comma before "although" | `she stayed although tired` | Concessive conjunctions after main clause need a comma |
