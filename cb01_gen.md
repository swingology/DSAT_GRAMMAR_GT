## Source question (for reference)

**Text 1:** Little is known about how plate tectonics—wherein slabs of Earth's crust move over, under, away from, and against one another—began. Some researchers contend that tectonic movements began around 3 billion years ago, often noting that computer models of Earth's mantle temperature at the time indicate that the mantle would have been sufficiently molten to enable the plates to move.

**Text 2:** Ultimately, any plausible claim about the inception of tectonic movement must rest on empirical evidence from the geological record. Researcher Wriju Chowdhury and his team analyzed the geochemistry of zircon crystals to gain insight into the chemical composition of the magma from which the crystals formed and, based on the data, compellingly argue that plate tectonics may have been occurring as early as 4.2 billion years ago.

Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to what "some researchers contend" as described in Text 1?

- A) By suggesting that the temperature of Earth's mantle 3 billion years ago was likely insufficient to allow for the level of tectonic movement predicted by computer models
- B) By distinguishing between computer models of Earth's mantle temperature that reliably predict the onset of plate tectonics and those that do not
- C) By indicating that computer models of Earth's mantle temperature are still being improved such that new models tend to be much more reliable than their predecessors
- **D) By asserting that a more definitive form of evidence than the computer models suggests a different timeline for the onset of plate tectonics on Earth** ✔

Correct answer abstracts both moves. ## Generated question

**Text 1**

The provenance of Earth's water remains unresolved. Some researchers contend that the bulk of it arrived late, roughly 4 billion years ago, delivered by icy comets flung toward the Sun during a period of orbital instability among the giant planets. They point to dynamical simulations of the early solar system in which such a bombardment readily supplies an ocean's worth of water to the inner planets.

**Text 2**

Whether a scenario could have unfolded is a separate question from whether it did. Cosmochemist Laurette Piani and colleagues measured hydrogen isotope ratios in enstatite chondrites—meteorites thought to resemble the rocky material from which Earth accreted—and found that these supposedly dry building blocks held enough hydrogen to account for several times the volume of Earth's oceans. Earth, they conclude, was substantially wet from its assembly rather than hydrated by later impacts.

**Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to what "some researchers contend" as described in Text 1?**

- A) By arguing that the period of orbital instability among the giant planets most likely occurred before, rather than after, Earth had finished forming
- B) By distinguishing between dynamical simulations that account for the hydrogen content of enstatite chondrites and those that do not
- C) By suggesting that once the hydrogen isotope ratios of comets are measured directly, they will likely prove consistent with those of Earth's oceans
- D) By contending that a study of the kind of material from which Earth was assembled points to an earlier and more intrinsic source of its water than the bombardment scenario allows

**Correct answer: D**

---

## Explanation (DSAT style)

**Choice D is the best answer** because it reflects how the author of Text 2 would most likely respond to the researchers described in Text 1. Text 1 presents the contention that Earth's water arrived late, about 4 billion years ago, via cometary bombardment, and notes that this contention rests on dynamical simulations—models of what *could* have happened. The author of Text 2 opens by drawing exactly that distinction: whether a scenario *could* have unfolded is separate from whether it *did*. Text 2 then cites Piani and colleagues, who measured hydrogen isotopes in enstatite chondrites (material resembling Earth's actual building blocks) and found enough hydrogen to account for the oceans several times over, concluding that Earth was wet from its assembly rather than hydrated by later impacts. The author of Text 2 would therefore treat a direct study of Earth's source material as more decisive than a simulation, and would point out that it indicates an earlier origin (at formation, not 4 billion years ago) and a more intrinsic source (the building blocks themselves, not comets) than the bombardment scenario proposes.

**Choice A is incorrect** because the author of Text 2 makes no claim about when the orbital instability among the giant planets occurred. Text 2 does not dispute the timing or occurrence of the instability described in Text 1; it disputes whether that event is the source of Earth's water at all. A student who notices that Text 2 argues for an *earlier* origin may be tempted to relocate the instability earlier, but Text 2 never addresses the instability.

**Choice B is incorrect** because the author of Text 2 does not sort dynamical simulations into more and less reliable kinds. Text 2's opening sentence frames simulations as a class—descriptions of possibility rather than evidence of actuality—and offers a measurement, not a better simulation, as the basis for its conclusion. The author would not concede that some simulations settle the question.

**Choice C is incorrect** because the author of Text 2 makes no prediction about cometary isotope ratios and, more importantly, would not expect direct measurement to vindicate the cometary scenario. Text 2's own direct measurements support a non-cometary source. This choice borrows Text 2's preference for measurement but attaches it to Text 1's conclusion, reversing the author's position.

## Generated question

**Text 1**

The question of when humans first entered the Americas has long been tied to the ice sheets that covered much of North America during the last glacial period. Some researchers contend that the first arrivals came roughly 14,000 to 16,000 years ago, walking south from Beringia through a corridor that opened between the Cordilleran and Laurentide ice sheets as they retreated. Paleoclimate models of the corridor's interior, which reconstruct vegetation from simulated temperature and precipitation, suggest that by about 14,000 years ago the passage would have supported the plants and game animals a migrating population would have needed, and these researchers treat that threshold as the earliest realistic date for a southward journey.

**Text 2**

A reconstruction can establish when a route became habitable; it cannot establish that anyone used it, or that no one had arrived by other means beforehand. At White Sands in New Mexico, Matthew Bennett and colleagues excavated dozens of human footprints preserved in the sediments of an ancient lakeshore. Seeds of an aquatic plant embedded in the layers directly above and below the prints yielded radiocarbon dates between 21,000 and 23,000 years ago, a span during which the ice sheets stood at their greatest extent and any interior corridor would have been sealed. The prints, Bennett's team concludes, show that people were living well south of the ice thousands of years before the corridor could have admitted them.

**Based on the texts, how would the author of Text 2 most likely respond to what "some researchers contend" as described in Text 1?**

- A) By arguing that the corridor between the Cordilleran and Laurentide ice sheets most likely opened several thousand years earlier than the paleoclimate models indicate
- B) By distinguishing between paleoclimate reconstructions that are calibrated against radiocarbon-dated plant remains and those that rely on simulated vegetation alone
- C) By suggesting that the people who left the footprints at White Sands most likely reached the site through the corridor once it had become passable
- D) By asserting that physical traces of human presence, unlike estimates of when a route became usable, indicate that people were already south of the ice before the corridor could have been traveled

**Correct answer: D**

---

## Explanation (DSAT style)

**Choice D is the best answer** because it reflects how the author of Text 2 would most likely respond to the researchers described in Text 1. Text 1 presents the contention that humans first arrived 14,000 to 16,000 years ago through an ice-free corridor, and notes that this date rests on paleoclimate models reconstructing when the corridor would have become habitable. The author of Text 2 opens by distinguishing what such a reconstruction can show (when a route became habitable) from what it cannot (that anyone used it, or that no one had arrived earlier by another means). Text 2 then cites Bennett and colleagues, who dated human footprints at White Sands to 21,000 to 23,000 years ago, a period when the corridor would have been sealed. The author of Text 2 would therefore treat the footprints, a physical trace of people actually being present, as more decisive than an estimate of when a route became passable, and would point out that they place humans south of the ice thousands of years before the corridor could have been used.

**Choice A is incorrect** because the author of Text 2 does not dispute when the corridor opened. Text 2 accepts that the corridor "would have been sealed" during the period the footprints were made; its argument is that people were present regardless of the corridor, not that the corridor opened earlier. A reader who registers only that Text 2 argues for an earlier arrival may wrongly extend that earliness to the corridor itself.

**Choice B is incorrect** because the author of Text 2 does not rank paleoclimate reconstructions against one another. Text 2's opening sentence treats reconstructions as a class that answers a different question from the one at issue. Although Text 2 does use radiocarbon-dated plant remains, it uses them to date footprints, not to improve a reconstruction of the corridor; the choice fuses Text 2's method with Text 1's approach.

**Choice C is incorrect** because it contradicts Text 2's central claim. The footprints date to a time when, according to Text 2, the corridor "would have been sealed," so the people who left them could not have arrived through it. This choice offers a common-sense reconciliation of the two texts that Text 2 explicitly rules out.
