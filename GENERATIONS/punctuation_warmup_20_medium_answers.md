## 1. C — correspondence, however:

The complete clause announces three subjects, and the colon introduces the list naming them. The parenthetical “however” takes a preceding comma. A leaves the list loosely attached by a comma; B puts a semicolon before a list rather than a clause; D puts the clause boundary before “however” and leaves the list without a complete clause of its own.

## 2. A — inlets,

The entire opening beginning “While” is dependent, including the relative clause about sailors. A comma closes that opening before “later surveys” begins the main clause. B incorrectly treats the opening as independent; C omits the boundary comma; D adds “but” even though “While” already establishes the subordinate construction.

## 3. D — terrace

The head of the subject is “channels,” and its main verb is “allow.” The intervening phrases do not justify punctuation between them. A, B, and C separate the subject from its verb with a comma, colon, or semicolon. The verb “form” belongs to the embedded relative clause, not the main clause.

## 4. B — transport—the

An em dash begins the interruption after “that,” so a matching dash must end it. Removing the interruption leaves “The researchers concluded that the storage method could preserve several varieties.” A mismatches the opening dash; C substitutes a semicolon; D leaves the interruption unclosed.

## 5. A — seasons,

The entire phrase from “a volunteer project” through “seasons” renames the Marsh Survey. Its opening comma needs a closing comma before “now includes.” B omits that comma. C uses a semicolon where no independent-clause boundary exists. D mismatches the opening comma with a dash.

## 6. C — entrance;

The first complete clause ends at “entrance.” “In practice” introduces the second independent clause, whose subject is “many visitors.” C correctly separates those clauses. A produces a comma splice, B fuses them, and D makes the second clause dependent with “although” after a semicolon.

## 7. B — stages:

The clause before the blank is complete, and the following phrases identify the two stages. A colon introduces that elaboration. A incorrectly uses a semicolon before nonfinite phrases. C leaves the elaboration without a separator. D creates a fragment beginning “strengthening.”

## 8. D — produced,

The first main clause begins “The digital catalog identifies”; the second begins “it does not indicate.” A comma before “but” joins them. A uses a semicolon in this ordinary coordinated construction. B omits the comma. C uses a colon before “but,” which does not introduce an explanation or list.

## 9. C — outside; nevertheless,

“Nevertheless” cannot join independent clauses with a comma alone. C places a semicolon between the clauses and a comma after the transition. A is a comma splice; B lacks the clause boundary; D incorrectly places a semicolon immediately after “nevertheless.” The later “while” clause belongs to the second main clause.

## 10. B — architect Lena Ortiz

“Architect Lena Ortiz” forms a single identifying expression within the subject. B keeps it intact. A inserts an unmatched comma between the title and identifying name. C puts a comma between the completed subject and “provides.” D places a colon inside the title–name expression.

## 11. D — growth:

The complete clause after the colon explains the “unexpected pattern.” A joins independent clauses with a comma, B fuses them, and C places a dependent “because” clause after a semicolon. A semicolon alone could also be grammatical here, but it is not among the choices.

## 12. A — gravel—

The explanation beginning “in this design” opens with an em dash and must close with one. A restores the underlying sequence “Water passes through a layer of coarse material before entering a lower chamber.” B and C replace the closing dash with a mismatched mark; D omits it.

## 13. B — as

The preposition “as” introduces the description “a gradual transfer of material.” No punctuation should separate them. A incorrectly treats the description as a colon-led explanation; C inserts a comma inside the integrated phrase; D puts a semicolon where neither side forms the required independent clause.

## 14. D — price, a

The phrase beginning “a policy” comments on the admission practice; it is not an independent clause. D attaches it with a comma. A uses a semicolon before a phrase, B creates a fragment, and C omits the separating punctuation. The verb “may explain” is inside the relative clause beginning “that.”

## 15. A — rusting, however;

Here “however” belongs at the end of the first clause: “The new coating was expected to prevent the metal from rusting, however.” A semicolon then separates the clause about cracks. B creates a comma splice. C places “however” at the start of the second clause without its comma. D supplies neither the transition comma nor the clause boundary.

## 16. C — donation,

The opening infinitive phrase runs from “To determine” through “donation.” A comma separates it from the main clause beginning “the archivist.” A and D incorrectly treat that introductory phrase as an independent clause. B omits its closing comma. The embedded “whether” clause does not change that structure.

## 17. D — include

The list directly supplies the objects of “include,” so no punctuation belongs between the verb and the list. A inserts an invalid colon; B and C make the same separation with a semicolon or comma. Contrast a complete introduction such as “The changes are substantial: …”

## 18. C — container. Inside

A period ends the clause about the first sensor. “Inside the container” introduces a new sentence with the subject “a separate device.” A creates a comma splice; B fuses the clauses; D makes the second clause dependent with “although” after a semicolon.

## 19. B — notice:

The main clause “The restoration has one limitation” remains complete even with the relative clause ending in “notice.” The colon introduces the noun phrase identifying that limitation. A uses a semicolon before a phrase; C supplies no separator; D creates a fragment. “Whose wooden frames…” is a relative clause, not a main clause.

## 20. A — leaves,

The noun phrase from “a shallow bowl” through “leaves” renames the collection’s oldest object. A closes that appositive with a comma before the main verb “was found.” B omits the closing comma, C introduces an invalid semicolon, and D mismatches the opening comma.
