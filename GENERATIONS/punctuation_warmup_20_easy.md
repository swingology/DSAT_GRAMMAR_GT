## Answer key and explanations

| Question | Answer | Main skill |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | B | Colon introducing a list |
| 2 | D | Comma after a dependent clause |
| 3 | A | No punctuation between subject and verb |
| 4 | C | Paired em dashes |
| 5 | D | Nonessential appositive |
| 6 | A | Sentence boundary before “meanwhile” |
| 7 | C | Colon introducing a noun phrase |
| 8 | B | Comma plus coordinating conjunction |
| 9 | A | Repairing a comma splice |
| 10 | C | Essential identifying appositive |
| 11 | B | Colon introducing an explanation |
| 12 | D | Paired em dashes |
| 13 | A | No punctuation between verb and object |
| 14 | C | Supplementary noun phrase |
| 15 | B | Repairing a comma splice with “however” |
| 16 | D | Comma after an introductory phrase |
| 17 | C | No colon between a verb and its object |
| 18 | A | Period between complete sentences |
| 19 | D | Colon introducing a list |
| 20 | B | Nonessential appositive |

**1. B — `threefold: to`.** The complete clause “the students’ goals were threefold” introduces a list of those goals. A uses a semicolon before an incomplete clause. C leaves a sentence fragment beginning with “To.” D fails to mark the introduction of the explanatory list.

**2. D — `Friday,`.** “Although” makes the opening clause dependent; a comma separates it from the main clause. A and C incorrectly treat that opening as an independent clause. B omits the separating comma.

**3. A — `greenhouse`.** The subject is “The wooden boxes beside the entrance to the school greenhouse”; its verb is “contain.” B, C, and D each insert punctuation between that subject and its verb.

**4. C — `volunteers—`.** The opening em dash before “built” needs a matching closing em dash. A substitutes a comma, B substitutes a semicolon, and D leaves the interruption unclosed. Removing the interruption gives “The new walking trail connects the town library to a small park.”

**5. D — `museum,`.** “The director of the town museum” renames Maya Chen and is enclosed by commas. A omits the closing comma. B and C substitute marks that cannot close the comma-delimited appositive.

**6. A — `bank; meanwhile,`.** Both groups perform actions expressed in complete clauses. A semicolon separates those clauses; a comma follows the transition “meanwhile.” B creates a comma splice, C fuses the clauses, and D incorrectly places a semicolon immediately after the transition.

**7. C — `experiment: a`.** The complete opening clause introduces the identity of “one more item.” The noun phrase after the colon does not need its own verb. A incorrectly puts a semicolon before that phrase; B produces a fragment; D provides no separator for the explanatory phrase.

**8. B — `Moon,`.** A comma and “but” join two complete clauses. A uses a semicolon before a coordinating conjunction in this simple two-clause sentence. C omits the comma. D incorrectly uses a colon before “but.”

**9. A — `bicycle;`.** “The students learned how to repair a bicycle” and “they also practiced replacing a damaged tire” are complete clauses. B joins them with a comma alone; C joins them without punctuation. D inserts an unnecessary comma after “and.”

**10. C — `quartz`.** “Quartz” identifies the mineral, and belongs to the subject of “appeared.” A separates the subject from its verb with a comma. B and D make the same error with a colon or semicolon.

**11. B — `project:`.** The second complete clause explains the plan announced in the first. A is a comma splice and C is a fused sentence. D places a dependent “because” clause after a semicolon. A period or a semicolon alone could also join these clauses, but neither is offered.

**12. D — `drills—`.** The supplementary phrase begins with an em dash before “ranging,” so it ends with an em dash after “drills.” A mismatches the opening mark, B omits the closing mark, and C substitutes a semicolon. The underlying sentence reads “The new program allows residents to borrow tools for small household projects.”

**13. A — `shows`.** The verb “shows” leads directly into its object, “the locations of public gardens.” B, C, and D improperly separate that verb from its object. A colon is not licensed merely because information follows a verb.

**14. C — `design, a`.** The supplementary phrase beginning “a feature” refers to the unusual design and follows a comma. A puts a semicolon before a noun phrase rather than a complete clause. B produces a fragment and repeats “a.” D omits the separator.

**15. B — `day; however,`.** “However” is a transition, so it cannot join two independent clauses with only a comma. B supplies the necessary clause boundary and the comma after “however.” A is a comma splice, C is a fused sentence, and D misplaces a semicolon after the transition.

**16. D — `paper,`.** A comma separates the introductory infinitive phrase “To compare the two types of recycled paper” from the main clause. A and B incorrectly treat that phrase as an independent clause. C omits the separating comma.

**17. C — `includes`.** The list is the direct object of “includes.” A incorrectly inserts a colon between verb and object; B and D make the same separation with a semicolon or comma. Compare Question 1: “the students’ goals were threefold” is already complete before its colon.

**18. A — `afternoon. A`.** A period ends the first complete sentence, and a capital “A” begins the next. B creates a comma splice and C fuses the sentences. D incorrectly places “and” after a semicolon in this simple two-clause construction.

**19. D — `contest: thin`.** The complete opening clause introduces a list naming the two materials. A places a semicolon before a noun phrase. B creates a fragment and repeats “thin.” C leaves the explanatory list without its introductory punctuation.

**20. B — `museum,`.** “A small local museum” is a nonessential appositive renaming Cedar House. The comma after “museum” matches the comma after “Cedar House.” A omits that closing comma; C and D cannot serve as its replacement.
