# ChatGPT review — Science and business inference questions

Reviewed September 8, 2026. Source: [inference_20q_science_business_traps.md](inference_20q_science_business_traps.md).

## Verdict

**Useful practice material, but not yet a polished medium-high-to-high difficulty test.** The science questions use worthwhile patterns: experimental controls, sampling bias, contamination, conditional models, and competing explanations. However, many correct answers closely restate the last sentence, and several wrong choices advertise themselves through sweeping language, direct contradictions, or invented facts. Technical vocabulary makes the passages look harder than the reasoning actually is.

**Science subset: approximately 6.6/10 for current item quality.** Q3 is the strongest science inference foundation. Q2, Q7, and Q9 are sound medium practice. Q1 and Q10 have strong experimental structures but need narrower conclusions. Q4 and Q5 need repair before scored use. Q8 is useful quantitative-evidence practice, while Q12 is easy trend reading.

The file contains **12 science and 8 business questions**; all 20 are reviewed here. Five items—Q4, Q8, Q12, Q15, and Q17—ask students to select table evidence supporting or challenging a stated claim. I would classify these as **Command of Evidence: Quantitative**, rather than standalone Inferences. College Board lists those as distinct skills within Information and Ideas. That leaves **9 science Inferences + 3 science quantitative-evidence items**, and **6 business Inferences + 2 business quantitative-evidence items**. [College Board content domains](https://satsuite.collegeboard.org/higher-ed-professionals/sat-validity/content-domains)

This review evaluates the supplied fictional passages internally, using the project's [inference evidentiary standard](../rules_refactor/rules/reading/skills/inferences.md). It does not validate the real-world scientific claims, official seed lineage, third-party research claims in Part A, or empirical difficulty. The proposed replacements below are suggestions, not changes to the original passages or answer key.

## Rating scale and overview

**Difficulty:** 1–3 easy; 4–5 medium; 6–7 medium–hard; 8–9 hard; 10 exceptional. Ratings describe expected reasoning demand, not measured student performance. For defective items, the difficulty estimate is provisional and describes the intended reasoning; ambiguity is not legitimate difficulty.

**Quality:** 1–4 substantial repair; 5–6 revision needed; 7–8 useful with targeted edits; 9–10 polished. Quality includes answer validity, uniqueness, distractors, and fit to the claimed task.

## Science questions

### Q1 — Coral bleaching

**B is the intended and best available answer, but it claims more than the experiment isolates.** Blocking a heat-sensitive protein reduces bleaching while ROS remain elevated. That implicates the signaling pathway; it does not establish that the protein directly detects temperature or that ROS cannot participate upstream. The passage also says the antioxidant neutralizes ROS without explicitly reporting successful reduction inside the treated coral tissue. A invents a failure, C conflicts with the observed ROS rise, and D is conspicuously absolute, so none is a strong competitor.

**Suggested B:** “the coral signaling protein contributes to heat-induced bleaching, and elevated ROS levels alone are insufficient to produce the untreated rate of bleaching when that protein is blocked.”

If retaining the stronger original mechanism claim, add measured confirmation of tissue ROS reduction and evidence that the protein responds directly to heat independently of ROS. For difficulty, replace D with a narrower but incorrect inference that bleaching tracks ROS concentration regardless of protein activity. Update Part C: pathway involvement is supported; the complete causal sequence is not established.

### Q2 — Finch sleep and learning

**B is uniquely supported.** Sleep and recorded calls differ together, so their contributions cannot be separated. The problem is instructional ease: the final clause explicitly names the confound. C's chance claim and D's unrelated scheduling proposal are weak.

**Improvement:** End the passage with “These results leave unresolved whether ______” and let students identify the confounding themselves. Replace D with: “equal tutoring exposure allows the difference in song accuracy to be attributed specifically to afternoon sleep.” This uses a real control but ignores the uncontrolled conditions. Light also varies; if the intended lesson is exactly two confounded factors, equalize lighting or acknowledge environmental differences in B. In Part C, label C unsupported rather than claiming chance has been statistically ruled out; no significance evidence is supplied.

### Q3 — Urban bird sampling

**A is a good inference.** Students must connect available food, willingness to enter a risky trap, and representativeness of the resulting urban sample. The point is possible selection bias, not proof that the populations are identical. D is the closest competitor but predicts an untested outcome. C reverses the meaning of boldness so explicitly that it is easy to dismiss.

**Replace C with:** “using the same feeder traps in both habitats ensures that any difference in measured boldness reflects a difference between the two populations.” This preserves the attractive standardization idea while ignoring differential selection. In B, remove the unsupported claim that urban birds entered more readily; make its single error treating the selected sample as representative. This is a strong candidate for further hardening without adding scientific jargon.

### Q4 — Bat calls and prey

**B is the most direct evidence, but C is also defensible support.** Frequency rises steadily from A through D. C says prey length does not steadily decline across that same ordering. A reader can legitimately combine C with the table to identify the same violation. The answer key cannot reject that reasoning merely because C does not repeat “frequency.”

**Replace C with:** “Mean prey capture height declines steadily from Species A to Species D as peak call frequency increases.” This is true but does not test the frequency–prey-size relationship. Retain B. Also change “primary determinant” to an explicitly monotonic prediction, such as “species with higher peak call frequencies consistently have shorter mean prey lengths.” A single exception challenges that rule; it does not by itself show frequency is not the primary determinant overall. Classify as quantitative evidence.

### Q5 — Methane source

**B is not adequately supported as written.** A microbial isotope signature identifies a source within the passage's stipulated distinction. It does not date production. Microbes could have produced the methane before the lake became isolated, with the gas subsequently preserved. Isolation prevents later surface input; it does not eliminate earlier surface input or earlier microbial production. Part C incorrectly treats all surface origins as ruled out.

**Minimal repair: replace B with:** “the methane's isotopic composition favors a microbial origin over the proposed sediment-release explanation, although it does not establish when the methane was produced.”

Alternatively, retain ongoing production as the target but add a constructed experiment: methane increases in sealed, living brine samples and not in matched sterilized samples under otherwise identical conditions. That supports present production in the tested samples, not uninterrupted production across 2,800 years. Revise both the conclusion and explanation accordingly. Replace an obvious distractor with “the microbial signature shows that methane production has continued without interruption since the lake froze.”

### Q6 — Smoke and bee visitation

**A is the best answer; the rescue experiment gives it meaningful support.** However, “chiefly” and “rather than directly harming” go further than showing that restored UV cues recover visits. The intervention does not independently measure physical harm, and unaddressed lamp effects complicate a strong mechanism claim. B and D use extremes; C introduces smoke-free effects without evidence.

**Suggested A:** “obscured UV cues contribute to reduced bee visitation under smoke, since restoring those cues can restore visitation even while smoke remains.” For stronger experimental isolation, specify comparable patches, random assignment, and lamps that restore normal UV patterns without changing temperature or other light. A useful wrong choice would attribute the recovery to reduced smoke concentration despite its being unchanged.

### Q7 — Urchin growth model

**B follows from the model's necessary conditions.** Low pH alone does not license a slowdown. A ignores temperature; C turns an offset into a net advantage; D imports adult biology. This is sound conditional reasoning, but B almost repeats the model verbatim.

**Improvement:** Give two sites that each meet only one threshold, then ask what additional change would make a slowdown eligible under the model. Keep “only when” logically distinct from “whenever”: necessary conditions alone do not guarantee a slowdown once both are met. If the intended answer predicts a definite slowdown under both conditions, explicitly make the model sufficient as well. Avoid treating current typical temperatures as a guarantee about all future temperatures.

### Q8 — Glacier lag

**A most fully supports the two-glacier timing claim.** B supports the same-year contrast but does not demonstrate Ravn's later response; C and D offer patterns compatible with delay without identifying the preceding warm year. The distinctions are reasonable, though Part C should call these incomplete support rather than completely irrelevant evidence.

**Improvement:** Add a fictional 2023 row with a modest summer anomaly and a large Ravn loss following warm 2022, then have A synthesize both completed lag comparisons. Currently 2022 has no following-year measurement. Narrow “registers the effect ... only” to a claim about the largest loss occurring later; a small observational table cannot exclude a same-year component or snowfall influences. This is quantitative evidence, not a standalone inference item.

### Q9 — Meteorite contamination

**B is the clear best answer.** Matching soil composition plus unsealed storage leaves terrestrial contamination unresolved. That prevents confident attribution to space without proving contamination. A combines an invented magnitude rule with certainty, C overgeneralizes matching values, and D predicts an unsupported counterfactual.

**Suggested B:** “the measurement alone does not establish a space origin because terrestrial contamination remains a plausible explanation.” Replace A with a narrower trap: “the matching excess establishes that all of the measured excess came from terrestrial soil.” This creates a useful possibility-versus-proof contrast. The scientific opening is accepted as a constructed premise here, not verified as a universal real-world rule.

### Q10 — Sorghum and microbes

**B identifies the important interaction, but “rather than on any direct effect” is too categorical.** Equal low moisture in sterile soil supports the absence of a demonstrated microbe-independent advantage under those conditions. It does not exclude smaller direct effects, combined mechanisms, or a measurement floor. Sterilization and the mutant strain also require care if the passage is meant to establish a uniquely isolated mechanism.

**Suggested B:** “the water-retention advantage associated with sorgoleone production appears to depend on microbes under the tested conditions.” Specify strains otherwise matched and, if necessary, a recolonized-soil control. Do not claim that recruitment of particular bacteria has been established without observing it. A should be criticized as lacking demonstrated independent support, rather than assuming every possible direct effect must have been detectable.

### Q11 — Sparrow dialects

**A is the best explanation under the passage's learning premise.** It connects early local learning to dialect persistence despite interbreeding. “Fixed,” however, implies permanence not expressly stated by “learn ... during a brief window.” The distractors rely on unsupported chronology, sweeping claims, or rescue speculation.

**Suggested A:** “local song learning early in life can maintain the dialect differences despite gene flow between the two banks.” If permanent fixation is essential, state that learned songs persist after dispersal. Replace C's “never ... any aspect” with the more attractive but unsupported inference that gene flow necessarily homogenizes learned songs. In the explanation, distinguish evidence against the proposed isolated-population account from proof that genetics never influences song.

### Q12 — Lizard body mass

**B is uniquely supported and easy.** The means increase across all four bands. A makes an invalid every-individual claim, C addresses sample size, and D is numerically false: 9.6 g is less than 11.3 g. The extra columns do not turn a direct mean-trend lookup into a hard inference.

**Improvement:** Say “mean adult body mass in the sampled elevation bands increases with elevation.” Remove the opening heat-retention explanation unless it serves the question; the table does not establish a mechanism. For medium difficulty, ask which conclusion the means and overlapping ranges jointly support: increasing average mass does not imply that every high-elevation adult outweighs every low-elevation adult. Keep the current version if easy quantitative practice is wanted.

## Business and economics questions

### Q13 — Four-day workweek

**A is appropriately qualified and supported.** A prior productivity trend weakens attribution of later increases to the policy alone. B invents motivation, C invents a reversal, and D asserts an unsupported counterfactual.

**Improvement:** Replace C with “the pre-adoption growth difference establishes that adopting the policy made no additional contribution to productivity.” This is a more competitive overcorrection. A can be tightened to “attributing all post-adoption gains to the policy requires accounting for productivity trends already present before adoption.” No post-adoption comparison with the prior trajectory is supplied, so neither benefit nor zero benefit is established.

### Q14 — Price anchoring

**A best connects theory and observed standard-plan uptake.** B is weaker, but Part C's dismissal is too strong: equal premium subscription rates do not prove identical subjective judgments of the premium price. B also resembles the stated theory's prediction for a lower anchor, making the explanation unnecessarily vulnerable. D assumes no premium subscribers, although equal rates are not expressly stated to be nonzero.

**Replace B with:** “visitors who saw the standard plan first subscribed to the premium plan at a lower rate than did visitors who saw the premium plan first.” That now directly contradicts the stated result. State that both groups had the same nonzero premium subscription rate if retaining D and its existing rationale. Describe A as the theory-consistent interpretation, not a direct measurement of perceived reasonableness.

### Q15 — Return-rate gaps

**B correctly captures the comparison:** footwear 9→3 percentage points, outerwear 7→3, accessories 3→1. A looks only at mobile rates; C only at desktop rates; D compares categories. These partial patterns do not by themselves specify the channel gap.

**Improvement:** Change the analyst's claim to “the mobile–desktop return-rate gap narrowed across all three categories between 2022 and 2024.” The table supports narrowing but does not isolate app improvements as its cause. For greater difficulty, use a category where both channels decline but desktop declines faster, and test whether students notice that the gap widens there. Such a change requires updating the claim and options, not just the numbers. Classify as quantitative evidence.

### Q16 — Surge pricing

**A is largely a paraphrase and should be qualified.** “Nearly all” redistribution and “virtually unchanged” totals do not establish that no additional drivers logged in. B and C directly contradict the counts; D speculates about duration.

**Suggested A:** “during the observed surges, the increase in drivers within surge zones consisted mainly of redistribution from nearby zones rather than a substantial increase in active drivers across the metropolitan area.” To add inference demand, ask which interpretation of a local driver increase is unjustified: treating local growth as equivalent to growth in the metropolitan total. Avoid claims about service in adjacent zones unless rider demand there is supplied.

### Q17 — Advertising lag

**A is the strongest support.** The two spending peaks precede the two highest revenue observations by one quarter. B and C omit the essential prior-period comparison; D focuses on a same-quarter increase. This has better repeated-pattern evidence than Q8.

**Improvement:** Phrase the claim as a pattern consistent with a delayed response. The table supports timing but does not prove ads caused the changes or exclude seasonality. Replace one weak distractor with a comparison that pairs a spending peak with revenue two quarters later, using accurate values. Keep the question focused on comparative support, and classify it as quantitative evidence.

### Q18 — Remote work and patents

**A is plausible but not adequately supported in its present strength.** Firms self-select into structured sessions, effort is not measured, and the sessions could influence effort as well as communication. Recovery in one group does not establish that lost interactions are the primary cause or that effort plays no role. All three distractors are weaker, but that does not repair the keyed conclusion.

**Suggested A:** “the recovery in patent filings at firms introducing cross-team sessions is consistent with reduced opportunities for exchanging ideas contributing to the earlier decline.” To support a stronger causal claim, use comparable firms randomly assigned to the intervention and a control activity matched for time and attention. Even then, exclude an effort pathway only if the evidence addresses it. Remove Part C's assertion that an effort explanation could not predict recovery.

### Q19 — Microloan eligibility

**A is a valid scope limitation.** Evidence from established business owners does not establish effects on people starting businesses. B predicts an untested group outcome, C invents less experience among waitlisted applicants, and D turns a sampling restriction into an exclusive effectiveness rule.

**Improvement:** Place eligibility in the methods description and remove “cautions, however” so students must connect it to the promotional claim themselves. Keep D's possibility-versus-exclusivity trap. The comparison also does not establish random assignment; do not rewrite the explanation to claim that the 22 percent difference is necessarily a causal effect among established owners.

### Q20 — Tariff employment effects

**A is intended, but its scope exceeds the reported evidence.** Across the two described sector groups, 1,000 added jobs versus 75,000 lost jobs gives a net decline of about 74,000. That is not automatically an estimate for the whole economy. Moreover, the passage explicitly attributes the losses to tariffs but does not similarly establish that tariffs caused the steel-sector gains. “Achieved their intended effect” is stronger than reporting gains during the period.

**Suggested A:** “the tariff-related job losses Montoya identifies in steel-using industries exceeded the contemporaneous job gains reported among steel producers.” If the target is the policy's net employment effect, explicitly attribute both estimates to the policy and limit the answer to those sectors. Use a fictional country and policy date if preserving the document's all-fictional framing; the current real-country/real-date presentation can make constructed estimates look like historical findings.

## Set-wide improvements, in priority order

1. **Repair validity before increasing difficulty:** Q4's competing answer, Q5's production-timing gap, and Q18's causal overclaim. Narrow Q1, Q10, and Q20 as well.
2. **Update the corresponding Part C explanations after revision.** Several explanations call an alternative contradicted or a mechanism ruled out when the passage merely leaves it unsupported. Best available answer and sufficiently supported answer are different quality checks.
3. **Replace conspicuous distractors with precise near misses.** Use the wrong control group, reversed necessary/sufficient conditions, unsupported exclusivity, or the wrong time comparison. Do not make two answers valid in the process.
4. **Remove answer-signaling endings where appropriate.** Q2 and Q19 explicitly announce the limitation. Their methods can carry that information without explaining its consequence.
5. **Correct the skill labels.** The five table items select evidence, and their stems are not completion-from-data stems. Preserve their usefulness without describing all 20 items as Inferences.
6. **Rebalance answer positions after editing.** The current key has 11 A answers, 9 B answers, and no C or D answers; Q16–Q20 are all A. Shuffle options and regenerate every related key and trap row together. Original answer positions were preserved in this review.
7. **Keep background claims clearly fictional or verify them separately.** A fiction disclaimer does not fix unsupported internal reasoning, and real locations, species, chemicals, and historical dates remain recognizable. No student should need outside scientific knowledge to reject an option.
8. **Replace the blanket hard-difficulty claim with editorial estimates.** As written, no item earns an 8–10 here. Build difficulty through inference distance and competitive distractors, then use student performance to calibrate it.
